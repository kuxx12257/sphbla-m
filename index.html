Research Note

Your Attention Mechanism
Can't See Distance

A provable geometric flaw baked into every transformer since 2017 — and an honest attempt to fix it, including the parts that didn't work.

By Kushagra Yadav · Read the full preprint →

Picture a lighthouse. Its beam sweeps out in a straight line, and anything sitting inside that beam gets illuminated — a rowboat twenty feet away, a tanker two miles out, doesn't matter. The beam doesn't get dimmer with distance. It doesn't know distance exists. It only knows direction.

Now replace the lighthouse with a transformer's attention mechanism, replace the beam with cosine similarity, and replace "illuminated" with "this token matters." You've just built a system that is, by mathematical construction, blind to how far away something is — it only ever asks "are you pointing the same way as me?"

For small sequences, this blindness barely matters. Nearby things and far things rarely line up in the same direction anyway, so the flaw stays hidden. But push the context window out to 128,000 tokens — the world GPT-4, Claude, and Gemini actually live in now — and the beam doesn't stay narrow. It widens. And widens. Until, at the scale these models actually run at, it's less like a lighthouse and more like every direction is lit at once.

This isn't a metaphor I'm reaching for. It's a provable geometric fact — and once you see it, you can't unsee it every time a long-context model confidently cites something from three hundred pages back that has nothing to do with your question.

That gap between those two numbers is not a rounding error or an edge case. It's the entire failure mode this post is about.

Why This Isn't Just a Curiosity

Here's the part that turns "huh, neat geometry fact" into "this might explain something you've personally experienced."

Cosine similarity, it turns out, has a nasty relationship with dimensionality. Two random vectors, projected onto a unit sphere in d dimensions, have a similarity score whose standard deviation shrinks as 1/√d. Push d up far enough, and the entire distribution of "how similar are these two things" collapses toward a single point: zero.

At d = 4096 — which is not a hypothetical number, it's the actual embedding dimension GPT-4-class models operate at — the standard deviation of cosine similarity between two random vectors drops to 0.016. That's attention losing almost all of its ability to distinguish anything by direction alone, at the exact scale where it matters most.

I call this the cone-widening problem: the region of "stuff attention considers acceptable" is a cone whose cross-section grows as 2·d·tan(θ) with distance from the query. Wider cone, more false positives, worse at exactly the scale everyone's racing toward.

Building a Flashlight Instead of a Laser

If cosine similarity is a beam with no distance falloff, what you actually want is something that behaves like a flashlight in a dark room — bright close up, and genuinely dimmer the further away you point it.

The fix I'm proposing, Spherical Biconvex Lens Attention with Magnitude Awareness (SphBLA-M), stitches two signals together that each solve half the problem and fail badly alone:

Signal one — direction, same as before: project everything onto the unit sphere, measure the angle. This part cosine already does fine.

Signal two — the missing piece: before you throw away the vector's raw length, take one more measurement first — how far along the query's direction does this token actually sit? That's the number cosine deletes and never gets back.

Combine them into a shape — not a cone, but a biconvex lens: wide right at the query, tapering shut as you move away in either direction, and asymmetric — a token sitting slightly behind the query gets penalized harder than one sitting slightly ahead, because directionally-opposed content is a much stronger signal of irrelevance than content that's merely a bit further down the same path.

The detail that convinced me this wasn't optional
I tried the angle-only version first, alone, distance term set to zero. It scored worse than plain cosine — 0.671 versus cosine's 0.837. Add the distance term back in, and the same setup jumps to 1.748 — more than double cosine's baseline. Neither half works alone. Together, they're the whole trick.

What the Math Actually Guarantees

I won't drown this post in derivations — the full paper has every proof — but three things are worth knowing because they're not hand-wavy claims, they're things I checked ten different ways until I couldn't break them:

It's smooth everywhere. The gradient never goes to zero, never produces NaN, across 3,000 randomized test points spanning dimensions 4 through 512. Analytic gradients matched hand-computed finite differences to within 0.6%.

It doesn't degrade with scale. SphBLA-M's discrimination between relevant and irrelevant tokens stays at 1.748 ± 0.001 — identical to three decimal places — whether you're at d=4 or d=512. Three orders of magnitude of dimension, same number.

It fails honestly where it should. On sequences with no actual structure to exploit — pure noise — SphBLA-M produces more diffuse attention than dot-product, not less. That's correct behavior, not a bug. A method that finds structure everywhere, even where none exists, is one you shouldn't trust.

Now, the Part Most Papers Would Quietly Leave Out

Here's where I could stop, and it would be a clean, satisfying story: identified a real geometric flaw, built a mathematically elegant fix, proved it works. Done.

But I don't think that's the whole truth, and I think the whole truth is more useful to you than the clean version.

Everything above was tested on a controlled synthetic setup — I built the "relevant" and "irrelevant" tokens by hand, with exact control over their distance and direction, specifically to isolate the cone-widening failure. That's good science for proving the mechanism exists. It is not the same as proving the fix helps on anything anyone actually cares about.

So I built two more tests, using real discrete tokens doing real reasoning tasks, not synthetic vectors:

ListOps — a benchmark from Long Range Arena where a model has to correctly evaluate deeply nested expressions like [MAX 2 9 [MIN 4 7 3] 1], tracking operators and values across long spans of tokens.

A needle-in-haystack task I built myself — one real, valid expression buried inside a pile of decoy expressions using the exact same vocabulary, so the model can't cheat by noticing "oh, this looks different."

The honest result
At the scales I tested, SphBLA-M shows no significant advantage over standard attention on either task (p = 0.73 and p = 0.95 — nowhere close to significant). At longer sequence lengths on ListOps, standard attention actually edges ahead, though with only 3 seeds that result is too small to lean on hard.

I think I understand why, and it's actually a useful finding rather than a disappointing one: ListOps needs exact structural retrieval — the operator determining the whole answer might sit far from where you're currently looking, and it's decisively relevant regardless of distance. That's close to the opposite of what SphBLA-M assumes. It was built for situations where distance and irrelevance travel together — retrieval, local coreference — not tasks where a far-away token might be the single most important one.

Testing SphBLA-M on ListOps was a fair test, and it came back telling me something precise: this fix has a scope, and I don't yet know its edges. I'd rather tell you that directly than let you find out by reading the fine print.

Where This Actually Leaves Things

Proven, solidly: cosine attention has a real, quantifiable geometric blind spot that gets worse — not better — as models scale up. That claim doesn't depend on SphBLA-M working at all; it's a fact about the math everyone's already using.

Promising, not yet proven: a fix exists that solves this exact failure mode cleanly in controlled conditions, with real mathematical guarantees around differentiability and scale-invariance. Whether it earns its keep on the messy, real tasks people actually train on is genuinely still open.

If you have intuition for why structural-retrieval tasks might resist this kind of geometric prior, or you want to try SphBLA-M on a task where you already suspect distance and relevance travel together — retrieval-augmented generation is my own best guess for where to look next — I would genuinely rather hear you tell me I'm wrong with evidence than have nobody check at all.
